📦 Logistics and warehouses

The most mature VLA application: general-purpose picking that scales to millions of SKUs without reprogramming.

The heart of the problem

Logistics is where ROI is already proven: picking assorted items, package induction, palletizing, and order picking. Amazon operates hundreds of thousands of robots and tests humanoids in its distribution centers. Covariant built manipulation policies — the Covariant Brain — that generalize to unseen SKUs, handling the huge variety of products (deformable, reflective, fragile) found in a real warehouse.

✓ What VLA does well

  • ✓ Generalize to new SKUs without retraining
  • ✓ Follow language instructions ("pick up the blue item")
  • ✓ Handling irregular packaging and clutter
  • ✓ Adapt the gripper to varied object geometries

✗ Still real limitations

  • ✗ Cycle time below that of experienced workers
  • ✗ Failures with highly deformable objects (bags, clothes)
  • ✗ Hardware costs remain high relative to productivity gains
  • ✗ Maintenance and downtime in high-throughput environments

🔧 Manufacturing and assembly line

The “flexible factory” without reprogramming: humanoids that adapt to new products on the same day.

💡 Why this matters now

Traditional manufacturing uses rigid robots that are expensive to program—each product change requires weeks of reengineering. VLA promises the high-mix, low-volume factory that adapts to new products without this. Figure and Apptronik are already testing humanoid robots on BMW and Mercedes production lines, performing tasks that previously required manual reprogramming with every product change.

🏥 Healthcare: assisted surgery, care, and rehabilitation

The highest-value sector with the highest barriers to entry—where autonomy is introduced one step at a time, with rigorous validation.

Gradual, non-disruptive autonomy

In healthcare, VLA appears in assisted surgery (systems like da Vinci gaining autonomy in subtasks such as suturing and cutting), care robots and assistance for older adults, hospital logistics, and rehabilitation with adaptive exoskeletons. The safety requirements are extreme: any failure can have serious consequences, so autonomy is introduced incrementally and always supervised by a human.

✓ Correct Approach

  • ✓ Human-in-the-loop for all critical decisions
  • ✓ Autonomy in isolated, well-defined subtasks
  • ✓ Extensive validation in simulation before OR
  • ✓ Regulatory pathway (FDA/Anvisa) planned from the start

✗ What not to do

  • ✗ Complete autonomy without immediate human override
  • ✗ Deploy before adequate regulatory certification
  • ✗ Test on real patients without a cadaver/simulator phase
  • ✗ Ignore anatomical variability across patients

🌾 Agriculture: selective harvesting, phenotyping, and spraying

The most challenging environment for robotics — unstructured, outdoors, and variable. Mastering agriculture proves technical maturity.

Three fronts in the field

A
Selective harvesting

Identify and pick only fruit at the right stage of ripeness without damaging the plant. This requires RGB+NIR vision, grip force estimation, and delicate movements among unpredictable branches. Startups such as Tortuga Agtech and Fieldwork Robotics are already operating at limited scale with strawberries and tomatoes.

B
Automated phenotyping

Measure plants’ physical parameters (height, leaf area, chlorophyll index) at high speed for genetic improvement programs. A robot does in hours what would take weeks in the field, generating datasets for selecting more productive and resilient varieties.

C
Precision sprinkling

Apply inputs (pesticide, fertilizer, biostimulant) only where needed, identifying target plants or regions through vision. Reduces input use by up to 80%, lowering costs and environmental impact. VLA-equipped drones are the first to achieve this level of precision at scale.

💡 Agriculture's unique challenge: extreme variability

Light changes from dawn to dusk. Plants grow and change shape. Wet vs. dry soil changes traction. Wind deflects trajectories. No simulator reproduces all of this with sufficient fidelity — agricultural robots need extreme domain randomization and out-of-distribution robustness beyond what is required in controlled indoor environments.

🛒 Retail and Services

The sector most visible to consumers—where VLA must combine manipulation, mobility, and human interaction.

📊 What VLA adds to retail

Combined manipulation + mobility

The robot needs to navigate among customers, pick items from shelves at various heights, and place them carefully. This brings everything together: perception, planning, manipulation, and social interaction.

Natural language instruction

A customer might ask, "help me find gluten-free flour" — the robot needs to understand the intent, navigate to the right aisle, and perhaps handle the box to show the item. The VLM is essential here.

🚧 Barriers to Industry Adoption

A technology working in a demo isn't enough—business, safety, and regulatory barriers determine whether it gets deployed in production.

The 5 real adoption barriers

1.
Uncertain ROI and payback — The robot costs US$150-300k (humanoid). The economics only work with very high throughput or unhealthy/dangerous tasks where no humans are available.
2.
Safety and certification — ISO 10218 (industrial robots), ISO 15066 (collaborative robots), FDA/Anvisa (healthcare). A long, expensive path, especially for critical domains.
3.
Legacy integration — Old factories, 90s-era ERP systems, sensors without an API. Integrating a VLA robot into the existing environment can cost more than the robot itself.
4.
MTBF and reliability — A robot stopped on a manufacturing line loses thousands per hour. Reliability must be comparable to industrial equipment (~99.9% uptime).
5.
Workforce acceptance — Fear of job displacement, need for reskilling, union agreements. The best technical robot may run into cultural resistance.

💡 Robots-as-a-Service (RaaS) as a solution

The RaaS model (a company pays per hour of operation instead of buying the robot) is removing the CAPEX barrier. Organizations such as Machina Labs and Covariant offer robotics capacity as a service, shifting maintenance and performance risk to the provider. It’s SaaS applied to physical robotics.

✓ Sectors with lower barriers to entry

  • ✓
    Logistics — Proven ROI, with no critical personal safety regulations.
  • ✓
    Agro — scarce workers, outdoor environments without the general public
  • ✓

  • ✗
